Module: Cloud and AWS Foundations

Before any service, two ideas decide how the rest of this track will make sense: where AWS physically puts your workload, and where its responsibility for that workload stops. Everything from Multi-AZ RDS to a locked-down S3 bucket is a consequence of those two.

Knowledge Library → Courses → Cloud AWS track → Cloud and AWS Foundations

What this module covers

This module frames the whole track. It starts with the service models — IaaS, PaaS, SaaS and the serverless slice that cuts across them — and the economics that actually drive cloud adoption, including the honest counter-case that cloud is not automatically cheaper. It then covers AWS global infrastructure properly, because Regions and Availability Zones underpin every availability decision in the eleven modules that follow.

The second half is about the boundary between you and AWS. The shared responsibility model tells you what AWS secures and what you must secure yourself, and that line moves depending on whether you run EC2, S3 or Lambda. It closes on account hygiene — the root user, an admin identity that is not the root user, multi-account structure, tagging, forgotten resources in forgotten Regions, and the cost controls that keep a learning account from turning into a bill you did not expect.

Topics

Cloud service models: IaaS, PaaS, SaaS — and where serverless sits

The service models are a ladder of how much of the stack somebody else operates. With infrastructure as a service you rent virtual machines, disks and networks and remain responsible for the operating system upwards — EC2, EBS and VPC are the AWS examples, and they behave like a data centre you can create with an API call. With platform as a service the provider runs the operating system and runtime and you supply an application — Elastic Beanstalk, App Runner and RDS sit here, since nobody hands you an SSH session into an RDS host. With software as a service you consume a finished application and configure nothing below it; Amazon WorkMail, QuickSight and, from the consumer's side, most of the SaaS your company already pays for.

Serverless is not a fourth rung so much as a billing and operations model that cuts across the ladder: no instance to size, no capacity to pre-provision, scale to zero, and payment for invocations or requests rather than for uptime. Lambda, Fargate, S3, DynamoDB, SQS and EventBridge are all described as serverless even though they occupy very different layers. The useful question in an interview is never "which acronym is this?" but "who patches it, who scales it, and what am I billed for when nothing is happening?" — the answers to those three questions place any AWS service on the ladder without memorisation.

The economics: capital expenditure to operating expenditure, elasticity — and the counter-case

The classic argument is a balance-sheet one. On premises you buy servers years before you need them, depreciate them whether or not they are busy, and size for the worst hour of the year; in the cloud you pay by the hour or the request, which turns a capital expense into an operating expense and frees cash that a growing business would rather spend on people. Layered on that is elasticity — the ability to run forty instances during a festival sale and four at three in the morning — plus the option value of trying a managed database for a week and deleting it if the idea fails. Neither of those is available when the asset is already bolted into a rack.

The honest counter-case is that cloud is not automatically cheaper, and a senior interviewer will expect you to say so. Steady, predictable, high-utilisation workloads are frequently cheaper on owned hardware, which is the substance of the a16z "trillion dollar paradox" argument about repatriation. Cloud bills are also full of costs that have no on-premises equivalent line item: cross-Region and inter-AZ data transfer, NAT Gateway processing, snapshots and volumes nobody deleted, per-hour charges for public IPv4 addresses. Cloud buys you speed, elasticity and the removal of undifferentiated heavy lifting — the way to defend it is on time-to-market and on total cost of ownership including the staff you no longer need to run a data centre, not on a naive per-hour comparison against a depreciated server.

Regions and Availability Zones: the two words the rest of the track depends on

A Region is an independent geographic area — ap-south-1 in Mumbai, ap-south-2 in Hyderabad, eu-west-1 in Ireland — with its own set of Availability Zones and its own copy of nearly every service. Regions are AWS's strongest fault-isolation boundary: they are deliberately built not to share control planes, so an event in one Region is designed not to propagate to another. That isolation is also why nothing crosses a Region boundary by accident. An S3 bucket, a VPC, an EBS snapshot and a KMS key all live in one Region, and if you want a copy elsewhere you must explicitly replicate it.

An Availability Zone is one or more physically separate data centres inside a Region, with independent power, cooling and physical security, far enough apart that a flood or a substation failure is unlikely to take out two of them, yet linked by dedicated redundant fibre with latency low enough for synchronous replication. New Regions are built with at least three. The consequence you will use constantly: spreading across AZs is cheap and nearly transparent — same VPC, same subnet routing, same low latency — which is why Multi-AZ is the default rather than an advanced technique. One detail worth memorising: the AZ name you see (ap-south-1a) is randomised per AWS account so that customers do not all crowd into the same physical zone, while the AZ ID (aps1-az1) is stable across accounts and is what you compare when you share subnets or debug cross-account placement.

Region  ap-south-1  (Mumbai)  -- independent geography, own control plane
  |
  +-- AZ ap-south-1a  (AZ ID aps1-az1)  one or more discrete data centres
  +-- AZ ap-south-1b  (AZ ID aps1-az2)  independent power / cooling / network
  +-- AZ ap-south-1c  (AZ ID aps1-az3)  redundant private fibre between zones

Why Multi-AZ is the baseline and multi-Region is a much bigger step

Multi-AZ is the default high-availability pattern because AWS has already done the hard part. The zones are close enough for synchronous replication, they sit inside one VPC, and the managed services expose the pattern as a checkbox: an Auto Scaling group across three subnets in three AZs, an Application Load Balancer with targets in each, RDS with Multi-AZ enabled, S3 replicating across zones invisibly. You pay a modest premium in redundant capacity and inter-AZ traffic and you survive the loss of a data centre with no application changes. The design principle to state in an interview is static stability: pre-provision the capacity to survive a zone loss rather than relying on a control plane to scale up during the failure, because control planes are exactly what is stressed during a large event.

Multi-Region is a different order of problem, and treating it as "Multi-AZ but further" is the mistake interviewers probe for. Distances mean tens or hundreds of milliseconds of latency, so synchronous writes stop being viable and you inherit asynchronous replication, replication lag, conflict resolution and a real recovery-point objective instead of zero. You need a data strategy per data store, cross-Region routing (Route 53 or Global Accelerator), duplicated deployment pipelines, duplicated observability, and regular failover testing or the standby is a fiction. AWS's own guidance is to get Multi-AZ genuinely right first, and to reach for multi-Region only when the workload's resilience or data residency requirements truly demand it — or when a regulator does.

Edge locations, Local Zones, and how you actually choose a Region

Beyond Regions and AZs, AWS operates a much larger edge network — hundreds of points of presence used by CloudFront, Route 53, AWS Global Accelerator and AWS WAF. An edge location is not a miniature Region: you cannot launch an EC2 instance in one. It caches and terminates connections close to users, which cuts round-trip time for static assets and, via Global Accelerator, gets dynamic traffic onto the AWS backbone sooner. Local Zones sit between the two — an extension of a parent Region placing compute and storage in a metropolitan area (several Indian cities among them) for workloads that need single-digit-millisecond latency to local users, with the control plane still living in the parent Region. AWS Outposts and Wavelength extend the same idea into your own data centre and into telco networks.

Region choice is then a four-way trade-off, and it is a common design-round question. Latency: pick the Region nearest the majority of your users, and measure rather than assume. Service availability: not every service and not every instance family launches everywhere at once, so check the regional services list before committing an architecture. Cost: per-hour and per-GB prices genuinely differ by Region, and data transfer between Regions is billed. Data residency and compliance: India's DPDP Act, RBI directions on payment data, GDPR and sector rules can mandate that specific data never leaves a jurisdiction, which decides the Region before any of the other three get a vote. Note also that a handful of services are global rather than regional — IAM, Route 53, CloudFront, Organizations and billing — and several of their control planes are homed in us-east-1, a detail that matters when you plan for a Region-level event.

The shared responsibility model: AWS secures the cloud, you secure what you put in it

AWS is responsible for security of the cloud: the physical facilities, the hardware, the hypervisor, the storage substrate, the network backbone, and the managed software of its own services. You are responsible for security in the cloud: your data, who can reach it, how it is encrypted, your identity configuration, your network rules, and whatever code and packages you deploy. Two things are never delegated no matter which service you pick — the classification and protection of your data, and the IAM permissions that decide who may touch it. Almost every headline "AWS breach" is in fact a customer-side misconfiguration on one of those two axes.

The important nuance is that the line moves per service. On EC2, an IaaS service, AWS stops at the hypervisor: you patch the guest operating system, harden the AMI, manage SSH or Session Manager access, configure security groups and encrypt EBS volumes. On S3, a managed service, there is no operating system to patch — AWS runs everything below the API, and your entire responsibility collapses into access configuration and data handling: Block Public Access, bucket policies, encryption choice, versioning, and object lifecycle. On Lambda, AWS additionally owns the runtime and its patching, so what remains yours is the function code and its dependencies, the execution role's permissions, secrets handling, and VPC or timeout configuration. Say it as a sentence in an interview: the more managed the service, the less of the stack you secure — but never less than your data and your IAM.

Account hygiene: lock the root user, then never use it

A new AWS account arrives with one identity — the root user, the email address you signed up with. It has unrestricted power, it cannot be constrained by IAM policies, and its credentials cannot be recovered if they leak into a public repository; an exposed root credential is an owned account. The day-one checklist is short and non-negotiable: set a long unique password, enable multi-factor authentication on root (AWS now requires it for management accounts), delete any root access keys — the console will not create them for you and there is essentially no legitimate reason to have one — and set the account's alternate security and billing contacts so alerts do not go to one person's inbox.

Then create a separate administrative identity and use that instead. The modern answer is IAM Identity Center (the successor to AWS SSO) issuing short-lived credentials to a permission set; the older answer, still acceptable for a personal learning account, is an IAM user in an administrators group with its own MFA. Keep the root credentials in a password manager or a sealed envelope for the handful of tasks that genuinely require them — closing the account, changing the account name or root email, changing the support plan, recovering from a bucket policy that locked everyone out, and a few billing operations — and treat any other use of root as an incident to explain.

Organizations, multi-account structure and a tagging strategy

Beyond a learning account, the industry default is not one account with careful IAM but many accounts with an organisation around them. AWS Organizations gives you a management account, member accounts grouped into organisational units, consolidated billing across all of them, and service control policies — organisation-wide guardrails that set the maximum permissions an account can ever have. An SCP never grants access; it only bounds what IAM inside the account is allowed to grant, which is what makes it the right tool for "no one in this organisation may launch resources outside approved Regions" or "nobody may disable CloudTrail". The usual starting shape is separate accounts for production, staging and development plus a dedicated security and logging account, and AWS Control Tower automates standing that up as a landing zone. At this stage you need awareness, not implementation — the detail belongs to the IAM module and beyond.

Tagging is the other governance habit worth forming now, because retrofitting it across a live estate is miserable. Agree a small mandatory set of keys — owner, environment, application, cost-centre — decide the exact casing and stick to it (tag keys and values are case-sensitive, so Env and env are two different tags and will split your cost reports), enforce them with tag policies or SCPs, and activate the ones you care about as cost allocation tags in the billing console so they show up in Cost Explorer. Tags are also how you find the resources you forgot: combined with Resource Groups and Tag Editor they answer "what is running, who owns it, and can we delete it?" — the question behind most cloud cost clean-ups.

  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [{
    "Sid": "DenyResourcesOutsideApprovedRegions",
    "Effect": "Deny",
    "NotAction": ["iam:*", "organizations:*", "route53:*",
                  "cloudfront:*", "support:*", "budgets:*"],
    "Resource": "*",
    "Condition": {
      "StringNotEquals": {
        "aws:RequestedRegion": ["ap-south-1", "ap-south-2"]
      }
    }
  }]
}

The free tier's real limits, and the billing alarm you set on day one

The free tier is the single biggest source of unpleasant surprises for people learning AWS, because it is neither uniform nor permanent. Historically it came in three shapes — twelve-month allowances for new accounts, always-free allowances that never expire, and short trials — and AWS has since reshaped new-account onboarding around credits and a free account plan. Whatever the current shape, treat the FAQ as the authority rather than a blog post, and internalise the two failure modes: services that were never in the free tier at all (NAT Gateway, most load balancers, public IPv4 addresses now billed per hour, larger instance families), and free-tier resources that silently exceed their allowance because you left two instances running instead of one.

The defence is a billing alarm created before you launch anything, and there are two gotchas that trip up nearly everyone. First, the EstimatedCharges metric only exists in the us-east-1 Region, so the alarm must be created there no matter where your workloads run. Second, billing alerts must be switched on explicitly in the billing preferences before the metric starts publishing at all. Pair the alarm with the free-tier usage alerts AWS sends when you approach an allowance, and get into the habit of opening the billing dashboard once a week — an unexpected charge caught on day two costs a coffee, and the same charge caught in week four costs a month.

# Billing metrics live only in us-east-1, and only after
# "Receive Billing Alerts" is enabled in Billing preferences.
aws cloudwatch put-metric-alarm \
  --region us-east-1 \
  --alarm-name estimated-charges-over-20-usd \
  --namespace AWS/Billing --metric-name EstimatedCharges \
  --dimensions Name=Currency,Value=USD \
  --statistic Maximum --period 21600 --evaluation-periods 1 \
  --threshold 20 --comparison-operator GreaterThanThreshold \
  --alarm-actions arn:aws:sns:us-east-1:111122223333:billing-alerts

AWS Budgets, Cost Explorer and finding the resources you forgot

A billing alarm tells you that money is leaving; Budgets and Cost Explorer tell you where it went and let you act. AWS Budgets sets thresholds on cost, usage, reservation coverage or Savings Plans utilisation, alerts on both actual and forecast spend — the forecast alert is the one that gives you warning rather than a post-mortem — and can trigger budget actions that apply a restrictive IAM policy or stop EC2 and RDS instances when a limit is breached. Cost Explorer is the analysis surface: filter and group spend by service, Region, linked account, usage type or activated cost allocation tag, and view the daily granularity that turns "the bill doubled" into "someone created a NAT Gateway on Tuesday".

Grouping by Region in Cost Explorer is also the fastest way to answer the classic beginner disaster: you were experimenting in a Region the console was not showing you, and something is still running there. The console only ever displays one Region at a time, so an instance in Ohio is invisible while you are looking at Mumbai. Cost Explorer grouped by Region names the culprit, Tag Editor and Resource Groups can search resources across all Regions at once, and the durable fix is the SCP shown above that denies resource creation outside your approved Regions in the first place. Cost work continues in module 10 — here the goal is simply that no account of yours ever quietly bills for something you did not know existed.

Interview questions

Foundations questions open almost every AWS round in Indian product and service companies — the Region-versus-AZ and shared-responsibility pair are effectively guaranteed, and the follow-ups separate candidates who have run an account from candidates who have watched a course:

Beginner What is the difference between an AWS Region and an Availability Zone?

A Region is an independent geographic area with its own control plane and its own set of AZs — Mumbai, Hyderabad, Ireland. An Availability Zone is one or more physically separate data centres inside a Region with independent power, cooling and networking, connected to the other zones by redundant low-latency fibre. Regions are isolated from each other by design; AZs are isolated from each other for failure purposes but close enough for synchronous replication. Nearly all resources are Region-scoped and never cross a Region boundary implicitly.

Beginner Explain the AWS shared responsibility model.

AWS is responsible for security of the cloud — physical facilities, hardware, hypervisor, storage substrate, network backbone and the managed software of its own services. The customer is responsible for security in the cloud — data, IAM permissions, network configuration such as security groups, encryption choices, guest operating system patching where one exists, and application code. Data classification and identity management are always the customer's, whichever service is used.

Beginner IaaS, PaaS and SaaS — define each with an AWS example.

IaaS rents raw infrastructure and leaves the operating system upwards to you: EC2, EBS, VPC. PaaS runs the platform and takes your application: Elastic Beanstalk, App Runner, RDS. SaaS is a finished application you only configure: Amazon WorkMail, QuickSight. Serverless — Lambda, Fargate, DynamoDB, SQS — is a cross-cutting model rather than a fourth tier: no capacity to provision, scale to zero, pay per request or invocation.

Beginner What is an edge location and how does it differ from an Availability Zone?

An edge location is a point of presence in AWS's content-delivery and DNS network, used by CloudFront, Route 53, Global Accelerator and WAF. You cannot launch compute or store your database there — it caches content and terminates connections close to users. An AZ is real infrastructure inside a Region where your instances, volumes and databases actually run. There are far more edge locations than AZs.

Beginner Why should you not use the root user for day-to-day work?

Root has unrestricted permissions that no IAM policy can limit, so a leaked root credential means a fully compromised account with no blast-radius control and no least-privilege story. Best practice: enable MFA on root, delete any root access keys, create an administrative identity through IAM Identity Center or an IAM admin user, and reserve root for the few tasks that require it — closing the account, changing the root email or support plan, and certain billing and lockout-recovery operations.

Intermediate How does the responsibility split differ between EC2, S3 and Lambda?

EC2: AWS stops at the hypervisor, so you own guest OS patching, AMI hardening, security groups, key or Session Manager access, and volume encryption. S3: there is no OS for you to touch — AWS owns everything below the API and your responsibility is access configuration and data handling, meaning Block Public Access, bucket policies, encryption and versioning. Lambda: AWS additionally owns the runtime and its patching, leaving you the function code and dependencies, the execution role, secrets handling and VPC or timeout settings. The rule: the more managed the service, the smaller your share — but never smaller than your data and your IAM.

Intermediate Why is Multi-AZ the default high-availability pattern, and what does multi-Region add?

Multi-AZ is nearly free architecturally: the zones share a VPC, latency allows synchronous replication, and managed services expose it as configuration — an Auto Scaling group across three subnets, an ALB with targets in each, RDS Multi-AZ. You survive a data-centre loss with no application change. Multi-Region protects against a Region-wide event and satisfies data-residency requirements, but the distance forces asynchronous replication, so you take on replication lag, a non-zero recovery-point objective, conflict resolution, cross-Region routing, duplicated pipelines and failover testing. Get Multi-AZ genuinely right before attempting it.

Intermediate How do you choose which Region to deploy into?

Four factors. Latency to the majority of users, measured rather than assumed. Service availability, because not every service or instance family exists in every Region — check the regional services table before committing. Cost, since per-hour and per-GB prices differ by Region and inter-Region transfer is billed. Data residency and compliance — India's DPDP Act, RBI payment-data directions, GDPR — which override the other three when they apply.

Intermediate Is my ap-south-1a the same physical zone as yours?

Almost certainly not. AZ names are randomised per AWS account so customers spread evenly across physical zones instead of all crowding into the first letter. The stable identifier is the AZ ID, such as aps1-az1, which refers to the same physical zone in every account. Use AZ IDs when you share subnets across accounts, compare placement, or reason about correlated failure between accounts.

Intermediate You set up a billing alarm and it never fires. What are the two usual causes?

First, the EstimatedCharges metric is published only in us-east-1, so an alarm created in any other Region watches a metric that does not exist there. Second, billing alerts have to be enabled explicitly in the account's billing preferences before the metric is published at all. A third, subtler cause: the metric updates several times a day rather than continuously, so a very short evaluation period on a fast-moving spend spike still gives you delayed notice — pair it with AWS Budgets forecast alerts.

Intermediate You inherit an account with an unexplained bill. Walk through how you find the spend.

Open Cost Explorer, set daily granularity to find when the increase started, then group by service to see what, and by Region and linked account to see where. Group by usage type to separate compute hours from data transfer and storage. Cross-check activated cost allocation tags for the owning team; where tags are missing, use Tag Editor and Resource Groups to search resources across all Regions, since the console shows only one Region at a time. Usual culprits: NAT Gateways, unattached EBS volumes and snapshots, idle load balancers, public IPv4 addresses, CloudWatch log ingestion, and cross-AZ or cross-Region data transfer.

Pro Which AWS services are global rather than regional, and why does that matter?

IAM, Route 53, CloudFront, AWS WAF for CloudFront, Organizations and billing are global; almost everything else is Region-scoped. It matters for two reasons. Resiliency planning: several global control planes are homed in us-east-1, so a large event there can affect operations elsewhere even though your data plane is unaffected — design for static stability and avoid needing a control-plane call during a failure. Governance: a global service is not partitioned by Region, so an IAM change or a Route 53 record change applies everywhere at once and needs correspondingly tighter guardrails.

Pro Design the account structure for a company with development, staging and production environments.

Separate AWS accounts per environment under AWS Organizations rather than one account with careful IAM — accounts are the strongest blast-radius and billing boundary, and they make "developer cannot touch production" structural rather than policy-dependent. Add a dedicated security or log-archive account for CloudTrail and Config data and a shared-services account for CI/CD and networking. Group accounts into OUs and apply service control policies as guardrails: deny disabling CloudTrail, deny resource creation outside approved Regions, deny leaving the organisation. Use IAM Identity Center for human access with permission sets and short-lived credentials, consolidated billing with cost allocation tags for chargeback, and AWS Control Tower to stand the landing zone up if starting fresh.

Pro Your CTO says the cloud will cut infrastructure costs by half. Do you agree?

Not as a blanket claim. Cloud converts capital expenditure into operating expenditure and prices elasticity well, so it wins decisively for spiky, uncertain or fast-growing workloads and for anything where time-to-market matters. For steady, predictable, high-utilisation workloads, owned hardware is often cheaper per unit — the substance of the repatriation argument. Cloud bills also contain costs with no on-premises analogue: inter-AZ and cross-Region transfer, NAT processing, per-hour public IPv4, orphaned volumes and snapshots. The defensible position is total cost of ownership including staffing, data-centre operations and opportunity cost, backed by right-sizing, Savings Plans or Reserved Instances for the steady base, and a tagging and Budgets discipline — not a per-hour comparison against a depreciated server.

Pro How would you stop engineers creating resources in Regions your company does not use?

A service control policy at the organisation or OU level denying all actions when aws:RequestedRegion is not in the approved list, with an exception list for genuinely global services — IAM, Organizations, Route 53, CloudFront, Support, Budgets — because those calls are made against endpoints outside your Regions and would otherwise break. Back it with detection rather than relying on prevention alone: AWS Config rules or Cost Explorer grouped by Region to catch anything predating the policy, and Tag Editor to sweep across Regions for orphans. Note that an SCP bounds permissions but never grants them, and it does not restrict the management account's root user, which is one more reason production workloads never live in the management account.
