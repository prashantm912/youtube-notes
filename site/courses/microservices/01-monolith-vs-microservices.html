Module: Monolith vs Microservices

Microservices are a trade, not an upgrade. This module sets the honest exchange rate for the rest of the track: what you gain, what you pay, and why the modular monolith is usually the right first move.

Knowledge Library → Courses → Microservices → Monolith vs Microservices

What this module covers

Before decomposing anything, you need a clear-eyed view of the deal on offer: independent deployability, independent scaling, team autonomy, technology heterogeneity and fault isolation — bought with network calls that fail, transactions that no longer commit atomically, an operations surface that multiplies, and a laptop that can no longer run the system. Martin Fowler calls the difference the "microservice premium".

We then look at the option most teams skip: a modular monolith, one deployable with enforced internal boundaries, which preserves the option to split later without paying today. The module closes on Conway's law — the observation that your service boundaries will end up mirroring your org chart whether or not you designed them to.

Topics

What microservices actually buy

A microservice architecture is a set of independently deployable services, each owning its own data, communicating over the network. The properties that follow are real and worth naming precisely. Independent deployability: one team ships a change to one service without a coordinated release train — usually the single biggest reason organisations move. Independent scaling: the checkout service can run forty instances while the reporting service runs two. Team autonomy: a small team owns a service end to end, with its own release cadence. Technology heterogeneity: the fraud engine can be Python while the ledger stays Java. Fault isolation: a memory leak in recommendations does not take the payment path down with it.

Note what is not on that list: microservices do not make code cleaner, do not make features arrive faster in a small team, and do not fix a bad domain model — a poor design distributed over the network is simply a poor design that is now harder to debug. Every benefit above is fundamentally organisational or operational, which is why the honest question is never "is this architecture better?" but "does my organisation have the problem this architecture solves?"

The distributed-systems tax, and when NOT to split

Every in-process method call you convert into a service call changes its failure model. It can now be slow, it can time out, it can succeed on the server and fail on the client, and it can return successfully while the caller has already given up. Business operations that used to be one database transaction become sagas with compensating actions. Reads become eventually consistent, so your UI must show data that is briefly wrong. Debugging needs distributed tracing because no single log file holds the story. Local development needs containers, stubs or a shared environment. And the operational surface multiplies: every service needs its own pipeline, monitoring, alerting, secret management and on-call rotation.

Fowler's MicroservicePrerequisites puts a floor under this — rapid provisioning, basic monitoring, rapid deployment, and a devops culture. The blunt version circulating in the community is "you must be this tall to ride". If your team is five people shipping one product, or your bottleneck is an unclear domain rather than release contention, microservices will slow you down and the premium buys nothing. The clearest legitimate signals are the opposite: many teams contending for one deployment pipeline, parts of the system with genuinely different scaling or availability needs, and a codebase that has become too large to change and release safely.

The modular monolith: the option most teams skip

A modular monolith is a single deployable unit whose internals are divided into modules with boundaries that are actually enforced — each module owns its domain types and its tables, exposes a narrow API, and may not reach into another module's internals. You get the design discipline of services (clear ownership, explicit contracts, no shared entity graph sprawling across the whole codebase) while keeping in-process calls, one database transaction, one deployment and one debugger. Enforcement is the part that distinguishes a modular monolith from a monolith with hopeful package naming: use Java's module system, ArchUnit rules, or Spring Modulith, which verifies module boundaries as a test and can turn cross-module calls into events.

The strategic value is optionality. Because module boundaries are already explicit, promoting one module to a service later is mostly a matter of replacing an in-process call with a remote one — and you will have learned, from a year of real change requests, which boundaries were actually right. Fowler's MonolithFirst makes this argument from observed project outcomes; Stefan Tilkov's counterpoint argues that a monolith built without boundaries will never split cleanly, which is precisely why "modular" carries the weight in the phrase. Both agree the failure mode to avoid is the distributed monolith: services that must be deployed together, which pays every cost and buys nothing.

com.shop
├── order/          // public: OrderService, OrderPlaced (event)
│   └── internal/   // Order, OrderRepository — invisible to other modules
├── payment/
└── shipping/       // listens for OrderPlaced; never touches order.internal

Conway's law and team topology

Conway's law states that organisations design systems which mirror their own communication structures. It is not advice; it is an observation that holds whether or not you plan for it. Three backend teams plus a separate database team will produce three services around a shared database, because that is the path of least coordination. If handing a change to another team takes two weeks, your architecture will grow seams exactly where those handoffs hurt — and interfaces will be sharp across team boundaries and mushy within them.

The practical consequence for this track: choose your team boundaries and your service boundaries together. The inverse Conway manoeuvre reshapes teams first so the desired architecture becomes the natural one to build. Team Topologies gives the vocabulary — stream-aligned teams owning a slice of customer value end to end, with platform, enabling and complicated-subsystem teams supporting them — plus the constraint that really governs service size: a team's cognitive load. A service should be small enough that one long-lived team can hold it entirely in its head, own it in production, and change it without asking permission. That, not lines of code, is what "micro" should mean.

Interview questions

Architecture-judgement questions from Indian product and service company rounds — the ones that separate a memorised definition from someone who has felt the costs:

Beginner What is a microservice architecture, in one sentence?

A system structured as a set of small, independently deployable services, each owning its own data and communicating over the network, organised around business capabilities rather than technical layers.

Beginner Name three concrete advantages over a monolith.

Independent deployability (ship one service without a coordinated release), independent scaling (scale only the hot service), and fault isolation (one service degrading does not take the whole process down). Team autonomy and technology heterogeneity are the other two commonly cited.

Beginner What are the main disadvantages?

Network calls that fail or time out, loss of atomic transactions across services, eventual consistency in reads, far harder debugging, heavier local development, and a multiplied operations surface — pipelines, monitoring, alerting and on-call for every service.

Beginner Is a monolith the same thing as bad code?

No. Monolith describes the deployment unit, not the internal quality. A well-modularised monolith can have cleaner boundaries than a badly split microservice estate. The usual complaint is really about a big ball of mud that happens to be deployed as one artifact.

Intermediate What is a modular monolith and why would you choose it first?

One deployable with internally enforced module boundaries — each module owns its data and exposes a narrow API. You get explicit ownership and contracts without network failure modes, distributed transactions or per-service operations. Choose it first because boundaries are cheap to move while they are still in-process, and you rarely know the right ones on day one.

Intermediate How do you actually enforce module boundaries in a monolith?

Make it a build failure, not a code-review convention: ArchUnit tests asserting no package depends on another module's internal package, Java Platform Module System or a multi-module build so internals are not on the compile classpath, or Spring Modulith, which verifies the structure in a test and supports module-to-module domain events.

Intermediate What is a distributed monolith and how do you spot one?

Services that cannot be deployed independently. Symptoms: releases coordinated across services, a shared database or shared entity library, synchronous call chains several hops deep, one service's schema change breaking others, and an end-to-end test suite as the only safety net. It pays every microservices cost and delivers none of the benefits.

Intermediate State Conway's law and give one practical consequence.

Organisations produce designs that copy their own communication structures. Consequence: if your teams are split by technical layer (UI team, backend team, DBA team), you will get layered components with painful cross-team changes, not vertical services. The inverse Conway manoeuvre reorganises teams around the architecture you want first.

Intermediate What is the "microservice premium" and who should pay it?

Fowler's term for the fixed complexity cost microservices add before any benefit arrives. It is worth paying only when system and organisational complexity are high enough that the productivity gains exceed it — many teams blocking on one pipeline, genuinely divergent scaling needs, a codebase too big to release safely. Below that threshold the premium is a pure loss.

Intermediate Your team of six is starting a greenfield product. Microservices or not? Defend it.

Not. Six people have no deployment contention to relieve, and the domain is least understood on day one, so any boundary drawn now is a guess that will be expensive to move. Build a modular monolith with enforced boundaries, deployed continuously, instrumented properly — then extract a service when a specific pressure appears (a scaling hotspot, a team that needs its own cadence).

Pro What signals genuinely justify extracting a service from a working monolith?

Release contention (teams blocking each other on one pipeline), a component with a materially different scaling or availability profile, a hard isolation requirement such as regulated or payment data, a genuine need for a different technology, or a change-hotspot that is already cleanly bounded. Notably absent: "the codebase feels large" and "microservices are best practice".

Pro What breaks first when a single database transaction becomes a cross-service operation?

Atomicity. You move to a saga: a sequence of local transactions with compensating actions for rollback, which means intermediate states are visible and compensation is business logic, not a database feature. That forces idempotent handlers, a transactional outbox so state change and message publication cannot diverge, and a UI honest about eventual consistency.

Pro How does team cognitive load determine service size better than any line count?

Team Topologies argues a service should fit within one long-lived stream-aligned team's cognitive capacity — they can hold its domain, code and production behaviour in their heads and change it without cross-team permission. Too large and ownership degrades into "nobody understands this"; too small and one team juggles a dozen deployables plus their operational overhead. Size to the team, not to a metric.

Pro An interviewer says "we moved to microservices and delivery got slower". Diagnose it.

Most likely the split followed technical layers or the org chart instead of business capabilities, so a typical feature now touches several services and requires coordinated releases — a distributed monolith. Secondary causes: shared database defeating independent deployment, no distributed tracing so incidents take far longer, and platform work (pipelines, observability, environments) never funded, leaving each team to reinvent it. The fix usually starts by re-drawing boundaries around capabilities and recombining services that always change together.
